# Spanish

Target language Spanish, native language English. Read this alongside
`references/levels.md`, which sets the text budget and the functions in play at
each level; this file maps those functions onto Spanish forms.

Contents: form inventory by level · focus patterns · glossing notes.

## Form inventory by level

Each level is cumulative. The "out" list matters as much as the "in" list —
level drift almost always happens by quietly importing a form from the row
below.

### A1

- **In:** present indicative. `ser`, `estar`, `hay`, `tener`, `gustar`.
  `ir a` + infinitive for immediate plans. Regular verbs plus the irregulars
  that are unavoidable in ordinary speech.
- **Out:** every past tense, subjunctive, relative clauses, passive, object
  pronouns beyond an occasional `me` or `te`.
- **Vocabulary:** family, house, food, weather, numbers, colors, routine.
  Cognates are welcome — they build confidence at exactly the point where a
  learner most needs it.
- **Sounds like:** *María vive en un pueblo pequeño. Tiene un perro negro. Todos
  los días camina al mercado con su madre.*

### A2

- **In:** pretérito indefinido and imperfecto, but in separate sentences rather
  than contrasted inside one. Pretérito perfecto (`he hablado`). Reflexives.
  Simple future. Direct and indirect object pronouns. Comparatives. `hay que`,
  `tener que`. `porque` / `cuando` / `mientras` clauses.
- **Out:** subjunctive, conditional, pluscuamperfecto, impersonal or passive
  `se`.
- **Sounds like:** *Ayer Ana perdió el autobús. Cuando llegó a la oficina, su
  jefe ya estaba enfadado. Le explicó todo y él la escuchó con paciencia.*

### B1

- **In:** pretérito and imperfecto in genuine contrast — this is the level where
  narration becomes possible. Future simple, conditional simple,
  pluscuamperfecto. Present subjunctive after the common triggers: `quiero que`,
  `espero que`, `es importante que`, `cuando` with future reference. `por` vs
  `para`. Impersonal `se`. Relative clauses with `que` and `donde`. Indirect
  speech in the present.
- **Out:** imperfect subjunctive, si-clauses beyond the real type, dense
  literary register.
- **Sounds like:** *Cuando era joven, Tomás creía que el río nunca cambiaba.
  Aquella mañana de invierno, sin embargo, descubrió que la corriente se había
  llevado el puente que su abuelo construyó.*

### B2

- **In:** imperfect subjunctive, hypothetical si-clauses (`si tuviera... haría`),
  the full compound tenses, passive with `ser` and passive `se`, discourse
  connectors (`sin embargo`, `a pesar de que`, `en cuanto`), subordination two
  clauses deep, reported speech with backshift, near-synonyms that differ in
  register.
- **Sounds like:** *Aunque le hubiera gustado quedarse, sabía que si no tomaba
  el tren de las seis perdería la única oportunidad que le quedaba.*

### C1

- **In:** the full subjunctive range including pluscuamperfecto de subjuntivo
  and the counterfactual si-clause (`si hubiera sabido, habría...`). Aspect used
  for effect rather than rule-compliance. Marked word order for emphasis. `lo` +
  adjective nominalizations. Idiom and figurative language. Regionalisms are
  fine when flagged in the vocabulary table.

### C2

- Near-native: wordplay, deliberate ambiguity, register shifting within a
  paragraph, literary and cultural allusion.

## Focus patterns

Each entry gives what the story must do structurally, the mistake an English
speaker reliably makes, and how the gloss should handle it.

### Past tenses — pretérito vs imperfecto

- **Force into the story:** a narrator remembering. Background in the imperfect,
  broken by a specific event in the preterite. Habitual past (`todos los veranos
  íbamos`) against a single occasion (`aquel verano fuimos`). Several sentences
  should carry both tenses at once.
- **Error:** English marks this with "used to" and "was ...-ing" or not at all,
  so learners default to the preterite throughout and narrate without texture.
  The mirror error is treating the imperfect as "the past tense for
  descriptions" and using it for completed events.
- **Glossing:** tag both — `told(impf)` vs `told(pret)` — since the English verb
  is often identical and the tag is the only visible difference. Render the
  imperfect as `used-to-tell` or `was-telling` where aspect is doing real work.

### Future and conditional

- **Force into the story:** a plan that meets an obstacle. Predictions
  (`llegará mañana`), promises, and the conditional as both hypothetical
  (`sería mejor`) and future-in-the-past (`dijo que vendría`). Include one
  conjecture use — `serán las tres`, "it must be three" — which surprises
  learners.
- **Error:** overusing `voy a` where the simple future fits, and missing that
  Spanish uses the future to guess about the present.
- **Glossing:** `vendría` → `[he]-would-come`; keep the future-in-the-past use
  literal and let the natural line sort it out.

### Conditionals and si-clauses

- **Force into the story:** a real choice. Real conditions (`si llueve, no
  salimos`), hypothetical (`si tuviera dinero, viajaría`), and at B2+
  counterfactual regret (`si hubiera sabido, no habría ido`). Getting all three
  in makes the sequence-of-tenses pattern visible as a system rather than three
  unrelated rules.
- **Error:** putting the conditional inside the `si` clause — *si tendría* —
  which English's "if I would have" actively encourages. The rule worth stating:
  `si` never takes the conditional or the present subjunctive.
- **Glossing:** tag the subjunctives, since the pattern is a pairing:
  `[I]-had(impf-subj) ... [I]-would-travel`.

### Present subjunctive

- **Force into the story:** someone wanting, doubting, fearing, or advising, so
  the triggers arise naturally. Contrast is essential — pair `creo que viene`
  with `no creo que venga`, or `cuando llega` (habitual) with `cuando llegue`
  (future). Without the indicative twin the subjunctive looks arbitrary.
- **Error:** English lost most of its subjunctive, so learners translate word by
  word and produce `quiero que vienes`. The productive rule isn't a trigger list
  but the idea that the subjunctive marks a clause as unasserted — wanted,
  doubted, not yet real.
- **Glossing:** `venga` → `[he]-come(subj)`. The literal line will read as
  broken English, which is correct; glossing `que` as `that` shows the clause
  boundary that licenses the mood.

### Imperfect subjunctive

- **Force into the story:** past-tense wanting and doubting (`quería que
  viniera`), polite requests (`quisiera`), `como si` + imperfect subjunctive.
  Pair with a present-subjunctive sentence so the backshift is visible.
- **Error:** leaving the subordinate verb in the present subjunctive after a
  past-tense main verb.
- **Glossing:** tag `(impf-subj)`; note the `-ra`/`-se` alternation in the
  vocabulary table if both appear.

### Ser vs estar

- **Force into the story:** the same adjective under both — `es aburrido` /
  `está aburrido`, `es listo` / `está listo` — where the meaning genuinely
  shifts. Location of things (`estar`) against location of events (`ser`), which
  reverses the usual intuition.
- **Error:** the permanent/temporary shorthand, which fails on `está muerto` and
  `es joven`. Frame it as identity and definition versus state and condition.
- **Glossing:** both are `is`, so the distinction is invisible unless tagged:
  `is(ser)` / `is(estar)`. One of the clearest cases for tags.

### Por vs para

- **Force into the story:** journeys and motives. `por` for cause, exchange,
  duration, and movement through; `para` for purpose, destination, deadline, and
  recipient. Get a minimal pair in if you can (`lo hice por ti` / `lo hice para
  ti`) — it settles the question faster than any explanation.
- **Error:** both are "for", so learners guess. Anchor `por` to what came
  before — cause, reason, route — and `para` to what comes after: goal,
  endpoint.
- **Glossing:** `for(cause)` and `for(purpose)`.

### Object pronouns and clitics

- **Force into the story:** dialogue and giving, where indirect and direct
  objects stack. Include `se lo dio`, attachment to infinitives and gerunds
  (`quiero dárselo`), and clitic doubling (`le di el libro a María`).
- **Error:** English word order (*di le*), and dropping the redundant `le` in
  doubled constructions, where Spanish requires it.
- **Glossing:** the literal line does the work here — `to-him it [I]-gave` shows
  the ordering directly. Hyphenate attached clusters: `dárselo` →
  `to-give-to-him-it`.

### Reflexives and the many uses of `se`

- **Force into the story:** true reflexives (`se lavó`), reciprocals (`se
  miraron`), meaning-changing pairs (`ir` / `irse`, `dormir` / `dormirse`),
  impersonal `se dice`, accidental `se me cayó`.
- **Error:** treating every `se` as reflexive, and avoiding the accidental
  construction in favor of a blunt direct statement.
- **Glossing:** distinguish by function — `himself`, `each-other`, `[one]`, or
  `on-me` for the accidental — rather than glossing every `se` identically.

### Commands and the imperative

- **Force into the story:** instructions, warnings, a recipe, one character
  bossing another. Affirmative and negative of the same verb side by side
  (`hazlo` / `no lo hagas`), since that is where the pattern breaks.
- **Error:** keeping the affirmative form when negating, and misplacing attached
  pronouns.
- **Glossing:** `hazlo` → `do-it`; `no lo hagas` → `not it [you]-do(subj)`,
  which shows why the negative looks like the subjunctive — because it is.

## Glossing notes

On top of the shared notation in SKILL.md:

- **Dropped subjects are everywhere.** Spanish encodes person in the verb, so
  most sentences need a bracketed subject: `Fue al mercado.` →
  `[He] went to-the market.`
- **Contractions** get hyphens: `del` → `of-the`, `al` → `to-the`.
- **Clitics** keep Spanish order in the literal line even though English cannot:
  `me lo dio` → `to-me it [he]-gave`.
- **Punctuation mirrors the Spanish**, including `¿` and `¡`.
- **Ambiguous `le`** → `to-him/her` unless context has settled it.
- **Weather and age idioms** stay literal: `hace frío` → `[it]-makes cold`,
  `tengo veinte años` → `[I]-have twenty years`. This is where the literal line
  earns its place — a learner who never sees the construction keeps producing
  *era frío* and *soy veinte*.
