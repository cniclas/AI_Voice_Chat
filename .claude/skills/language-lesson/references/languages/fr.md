# French

French as the **target** language, for a learner of any native language. Read
this alongside `references/levels.md`, which sets the text budget and the
functions in play at each level; this file maps those functions onto French
forms.

What a *particular* native language misreads in French is not here — that is
directional, and lives in `translation-review/references/languages/fr-<native>.md`
(`fr-es.md` ships). The one place the native language shows up below is the
glossing notes, because the literal line is written in it.

Contents: form inventory by level · focus patterns · glossing notes.

## Form inventory by level

Each level is cumulative. The "out" list matters as much as the "in" list —
level drift almost always happens by quietly importing a form from the row
below.

### A1

- **In:** present indicative. `être`, `avoir`, `aller`, `faire`, `il y a`.
  `aller` + infinitive for immediate plans. Definite, indefinite and partitive
  articles. Regular `-er` verbs plus the irregulars nobody can avoid.
  Adjective agreement in the simple cases.
- **Out:** every past tense, subjunctive, relative clauses beyond `qui`,
  object pronouns other than an occasional `me` or `te`, `y` and `en`.
- **Vocabulary:** family, house, food, weather, numbers, colors, routine.
  Cognates are welcome — they build confidence at exactly the point where a
  learner most needs it.
- **Sounds like:** *Marie habite dans un petit village. Elle a un chien noir.
  Tous les jours, elle va au marché avec sa mère.*

### A2

- **In:** passé composé and imparfait, but in separate sentences rather than
  contrasted inside one. Auxiliary choice (`avoir` / `être`) including the
  `être` verbs of motion. Pronominal verbs. Simple future. Direct and indirect
  object pronouns, and `y` / `en` in their concrete uses. Comparatives.
  `il faut` + infinitive, `devoir`, `pouvoir`. `parce que` / `quand` /
  `pendant que` clauses.
- **Out:** subjunctive, conditional, plus-que-parfait, passive, `dont`.
- **Sounds like:** *Hier, Anne a raté le bus. Quand elle est arrivée au bureau,
  son chef était déjà fâché. Elle lui a tout expliqué et il l'a écoutée
  patiemment.*

### B1

- **In:** passé composé and imparfait in genuine contrast — this is the level
  where narration becomes possible. Simple future, present conditional,
  plus-que-parfait. Present subjunctive after the common triggers: `il faut
  que`, `je veux que`, `bien que`, `pour que`. Past participle agreement with a
  preceding direct object. `y` and `en` replacing full phrases. Relative
  clauses with `qui`, `que`, `où`, and a first `dont`. Reported speech in the
  present. `depuis` with the present.
- **Out:** passé simple, imperfect subjunctive, dense literary register,
  `ne … que` stacked with other negations.
- **Sounds like:** *Quand il était jeune, Thomas croyait que la rivière ne
  changeait jamais. Ce matin d'hiver, pourtant, il a découvert que le courant
  avait emporté le pont que son grand-père avait construit.*

### B2

- **In:** the full conditional system including `si` + plus-que-parfait →
  conditionnel passé, the passive with `être` and its `on` alternative,
  discourse connectors (`cependant`, `bien que`, `dès que`, `en revanche`),
  subordination two clauses deep, reported speech with backshift, the gerund
  (`en marchant`), near-synonyms that differ in register. Passé simple may be
  read (in a narrated aside) but need not be produced.
- **Sounds like:** *Bien qu'il eût aimé rester, il savait que s'il ne prenait
  pas le train de six heures, il perdrait la seule occasion qui lui restait.*

### C1

- **In:** the full subjunctive range including the past subjunctive, aspect used
  for effect rather than rule-compliance, marked word order and dislocation
  (`ce livre-là, je l'ai lu`), `ce qui` / `ce dont` nominalizations, literary
  passé simple in narration, idiom and figurative language. Regionalisms are
  fine when flagged in the vocabulary table.

### C2

- Near-native: wordplay, deliberate ambiguity, register shifting within a
  paragraph, literary and cultural allusion.

## Focus patterns

Each entry gives what the story must do structurally, the mistake a learner
reliably makes, and how the gloss should handle it. Where an error belongs to
one native language rather than to learners generally, it says so.

### Past tenses — passé composé vs imparfait

- **Force into the story:** a narrator remembering. Background in the imparfait,
  broken by a specific event in the passé composé. Habitual past (`tous les étés
  nous allions`) against a single occasion (`cet été-là nous sommes allés`).
  Several sentences should carry both tenses at once.
- **Error:** narrating entirely in one tense, so the story has no texture. The
  mirror error is treating the imparfait as "the past tense for descriptions"
  and using it for completed events. *For Spanish natives:* the split maps
  closely onto pretérito/imperfecto and transfers well — the trap is the
  auxiliary, not the aspect.
- **Glossing:** the passé composé is two words and the literal line should show
  both: `il a mangé` → `he has eaten(pc)`. Tag both tenses — `told(impf)` vs
  `told(pc)` — where the aspect contrast is the point.

### Auxiliaries and past participle agreement

- **Force into the story:** movement and pronominal verbs alongside ordinary
  transitive ones, so `avoir` and `être` alternate visibly. Include one
  preceding direct object that forces agreement — `la lettre qu'il a écrite` —
  and one pronominal verb with a direct object after it, where agreement does
  *not* happen (`elle s'est lavé les mains`).
- **Error:** using `avoir` for the `être` verbs, and either agreeing everything
  or agreeing nothing. The agreement rule is invisible in speech for most verbs,
  which is exactly why it needs to be seen in writing.
- **Glossing:** keep the auxiliary as its own word — `elle est arrivée` →
  `she is arrived(pc,fem)` — and tag the agreement rather than smoothing it
  away.

### Future and conditional

- **Force into the story:** a plan that meets an obstacle. Predictions
  (`il arrivera demain`), promises, and the conditional as both hypothetical
  (`ce serait mieux`) and future-in-the-past (`il a dit qu'il viendrait`).
  Include one polite conditional — `je voudrais` — which is the use learners
  meet first and analyse last.
- **Error:** overusing `aller` + infinitive where the simple future fits, and
  building the conditional off the infinitive incorrectly for the irregular
  stems (`faire` → `fer-`, `venir` → `viendr-`).
- **Glossing:** `viendrait` → `[he]-would-come`; keep the future-in-the-past use
  literal and let the natural line sort it out.

### Conditionals and si-clauses

- **Force into the story:** a real choice. Real conditions (`s'il pleut, on ne
  sort pas`), hypothetical (`si j'avais de l'argent, je voyagerais`), and at B2+
  counterfactual regret (`si j'avais su, je ne serais pas venu`). Getting all
  three in makes the sequence-of-tenses pattern visible as a system rather than
  three unrelated rules.
- **Error:** putting the conditional inside the `si` clause — *si j'aurais*.
  The rule worth stating: `si` takes the present, the imparfait or the
  plus-que-parfait, never the conditional. *For Spanish natives:* Spanish uses
  the imperfect subjunctive here (`si tuviera`), so the French imparfait looks
  suspiciously indicative and invites a subjunctive that does not belong.
- **Glossing:** show the pairing — `[I]-had(impf) … [I]-would-travel` — so the
  tense on each side of the comma is legible.

### Present subjunctive

- **Force into the story:** someone wanting, doubting, fearing, or needing, so
  the triggers arise naturally. Contrast is essential — pair `je crois qu'il
  vient` with `je ne crois pas qu'il vienne`. Without the indicative twin the
  subjunctive looks arbitrary.
- **Error:** using the indicative after `il faut que` and `bien que`, and
  assuming the trigger list matches the native language's. *For Spanish
  natives:* most triggers do line up, but French uses the indicative after
  `après que` and `espérer que`, where Spanish takes the subjunctive — those two
  are worth putting in the story.
- **Glossing:** `vienne` → `[he]-come(subj)`. The literal line will read as
  broken, which is correct; glossing `que` as `that` shows the clause boundary
  that licenses the mood.

### Object pronouns, `y` and `en`

- **Force into the story:** dialogue and giving, where indirect and direct
  objects stack (`il le lui a donné`), plus `y` for a place already named and
  `en` for a quantity already named (`j'en ai trois`). Include one imperative,
  where the order flips (`donne-le-moi`).
- **Error:** putting the pronoun after the verb on the model of the native
  language, and reaching for a full noun phrase where French requires `en`.
  *For Spanish natives:* Spanish has no `y`/`en`, so they get dropped entirely —
  *j'ai trois* for *j'en ai trois* — and the doubled order `le lui` is the
  mirror of Spanish `se lo`, which invites the wrong sequence.
- **Glossing:** the literal line does the work — `il le lui a donné` →
  `he it to-him has given(pc)`. Gloss `en` as `of-it/of-them` and `y` as
  `there/to-it` rather than dropping them.

### Articles and the partitive

- **Force into the story:** food, shopping, abstractions — anywhere a mass noun
  appears. Contrast `du pain` (some bread), `le pain` (bread in general), `un
  pain` (a loaf), and the negative `pas de pain`, which is where the pattern
  breaks.
- **Error:** omitting the article where the native language does, and keeping
  `du`/`des` after a negative instead of switching to `de`. *For Spanish
  natives:* Spanish has no partitive at all — *je mange pain* — and uses a bare
  plural where French requires `des`.
- **Glossing:** gloss the partitive rather than dissolving it: `du pain` →
  `of-the bread`, `pas de pain` → `not of bread`. Smoothing it to "bread" hides
  the whole point.

### Pronominal verbs

- **Force into the story:** true reflexives (`elle s'est lavée`), reciprocals
  (`ils se sont regardés`), meaning-changing pairs (`aller` / `s'en aller`,
  `douter` / `se douter`), and the passive `se` (`ça se dit`).
- **Error:** treating every `se` as reflexive, and forgetting that pronominal
  verbs all take `être` in the compound tenses.
- **Glossing:** distinguish by function — `herself`, `each-other`, `[one]` —
  rather than glossing every `se` identically.

### Negation

- **Force into the story:** a character refusing, denying, or lacking something.
  Get the two halves of `ne … pas` around a compound verb (`il n'a pas
  compris`), one `ne … que` restriction, and one `ne … plus` or `ne … jamais`.
  Spoken-register dropping of `ne` can appear in dialogue only, flagged in the
  notes.
- **Error:** placing the second half of the negation after the participle
  instead of around the auxiliary, and reading `ne … que` as a negation when it
  means "only".
- **Glossing:** keep both halves visible: `il n'a pas compris` →
  `he not-has not understood(pc)`; `je n'ai que dix euros` →
  `I not-have only ten euros`.

### Relative pronouns and `dont`

- **Force into the story:** a person or object described across two clauses, so
  `qui` and `que` alternate, plus one `où` of time (`le jour où`) and one `dont`
  standing in for `de` + noun (`le livre dont je parle`).
- **Error:** choosing `qui`/`que` by meaning rather than by grammatical role,
  and avoiding `dont` entirely by restarting the sentence.
- **Glossing:** `dont` → `of-which/whose`, `le jour où` → `the day where`, which
  shows why French uses a locative for time.

### Time expressions — `depuis`, `pendant`, `il y a`

- **Force into the story:** a situation that has lasted, an action that took a
  while, and an event that happened a while back — all three close together, so
  the contrast is forced.
- **Error:** using a past tense with `depuis`, where French takes the present
  (`j'habite ici depuis trois ans`), and confusing `il y a` (ago) with `depuis`
  (since/for).
- **Glossing:** keep the tense visible — `[I]-live here since three years` —
  because that mismatch with the native language is the whole lesson.

## Glossing notes

On top of the shared notation in SKILL.md. These hold whatever the literal line
is written in; the per-native sections below carry the worked examples, because
a gloss only exists in one language at a time.

- **Subjects are never dropped**, unlike Spanish or Italian, so the literal line
  rarely needs a bracketed subject — but impersonal `il` and `on` do need
  marking, since they stand for nothing in particular.
- **Compound tenses are two words** and stay two words in the literal line: the
  auxiliary is glossed as itself (`a` → `has`, `est` → `is`), with the tense tag
  on the participle.
- **Contractions** get hyphens: `du` → `of-the`, `au` → `to-the`, `des` →
  `of-the`.
- **Elision** is undone silently: `l'a` glosses as `it has`, `n'est` as
  `not-is`. Do not leave an apostrophe stranded in the gloss.
- **Both halves of a negation** stay in the literal line, in French order.
- **`y` and `en` are never dropped** — a gloss that omits them teaches exactly
  the mistake the learner already makes.
- **Liaison and silent endings are invisible in speech**, which is why agreement
  has to be visible in the gloss: tag it rather than letting it disappear.

### Into Spanish

- Compound past: `Il a mangé du pain.` → `Él ha comido de-el pan.`
  (*Nat.* `Comió pan.`) — the partitive `du` survives in the literal line even
  though Spanish would never write it.
- `on`: `On dit que…` → `[Uno] dice que…`, not `Nosotros decimos`.
- `y` / `en`: `J'en ai trois.` → `Yo de-ello tengo tres.`;
  `Il y va demain.` → `Él allí va mañana.`
- Negation: `Elle n'a pas compris.` → `Ella no-ha no comprendido(pc).`

Three full worked sentences, showing what the two lines are for:

> **Quand j'étais enfant, mon grand-père me racontait des histoires du fleuve.**
> - *Lit.* Cuando yo-era(impf) niño, mi abuelo me contaba(impf) de-las historias de-el río.
> - *Nat.* Cuando era niño, mi abuelo me contaba historias del río.

> **Elle s'est levée tôt parce qu'il faisait froid.**
> - *Lit.* Ella se es levantada(pc,fem) temprano porque él hacía(impf) frío.
> - *Nat.* Se levantó temprano porque hacía frío.

> **Si j'avais le temps, je lui écrirais une lettre.**
> - *Lit.* Si yo-tenía(impf) el tiempo, yo le escribiría una carta.
> - *Nat.* Si tuviera tiempo, le escribiría una carta.

Note the third: French puts the **imparfait** after `si` where Spanish puts the
imperfect subjunctive. The literal line has to show the indicative, or the
learner never sees that the two languages differ here and keeps writing
*si j'eusse*.
