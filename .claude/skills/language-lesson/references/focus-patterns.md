# Focus patterns

Read the section matching the requested focus. Each gives what the story has to
do structurally, the mistake an English speaker reliably makes, and how the
literal gloss should handle it.

One principle runs through all of them: **contrast beats repetition.** A story
that uses the preterite twelve times teaches nothing about the preterite. A
story that puts a preterite and an imperfect in the same sentence, doing
different jobs, makes the distinction visible in a way no rule statement does.
Plot the story so the contrast is forced by events rather than sprinkled on.

## Past tenses — pretérito vs imperfecto

- **Force into the story:** a narrator remembering. Background scene-setting in
  the imperfect, then a specific event in the preterite that breaks it. Habitual
  past (`todos los veranos íbamos`) against a single occasion (`aquel verano
  fuimos`). Aim for several sentences carrying both tenses at once.
- **English-speaker error:** English marks this with "used to" and "was ...-ing"
  or not at all, so learners default to the preterite for everything and narrate
  a story with no texture. The reverse error is treating the imperfect as
  "the past tense for descriptions" and using it for completed events.
- **Glossing:** tag both — `told(impf)` vs `told(pret)` — since the English verb
  is often identical and the tag is the only visible difference. Render the
  imperfect as `used-to-tell` or `was-telling` in the literal line when the
  aspect is doing real work.

## Future and conditional

- **Force into the story:** a plan that meets an obstacle. Predictions
  (`llegará mañana`), promises, and the conditional as both hypothetical
  (`sería mejor`) and future-in-the-past (`dijo que vendría`). Include at least
  one conjecture use — `serán las tres`, "it must be three" — which surprises
  learners.
- **English-speaker error:** overusing `voy a` where the simple future fits, and
  missing that Spanish uses the future for guessing about the present.
- **Glossing:** `vendría` → `[he]-would-come`; keep the future-in-the-past use
  literal and let the natural line handle it.

## Conditionals and si-clauses

- **Force into the story:** a genuine choice. Real conditions (`si llueve, no
  salimos`), hypothetical (`si tuviera dinero, viajaría`), and — at B2+ —
  counterfactual regret (`si hubiera sabido, no habría ido`). Getting all three
  in makes the sequence-of-tenses pattern visible as a system.
- **English-speaker error:** putting the conditional inside the `si` clause —
  *si tendría* — which English's "if I would have" encourages. The rule worth
  stating: `si` never takes the conditional or the present subjunctive.
- **Glossing:** tag the subjunctives, since the whole pattern is a pairing:
  `[I]-had(impf-subj) ... [I]-would-travel`.

## Present subjunctive

- **Force into the story:** someone wanting, doubting, fearing, or advising, so
  the trigger verbs appear naturally. Contrast is essential here — pair
  `creo que viene` with `no creo que venga`, or `cuando llega` (habitual) with
  `cuando llegue` (future). Without the indicative twin, the subjunctive looks
  arbitrary.
- **English-speaker error:** English lost most of its subjunctive, so learners
  translate word by word and produce `quiero que vienes`. The productive rule
  isn't a trigger list but the idea that the subjunctive marks the clause as
  unasserted — wanted, doubted, not yet real.
- **Glossing:** `venga` → `[he]-come(subj)`. The literal line will read as
  broken English and that's correct; `que` glossed as `that` shows the learner
  the clause boundary that licenses the mood.

## Imperfect subjunctive

- **Force into the story:** past-tense wanting and doubting (`quería que
  viniera`), polite requests (`quisiera`), and `como si` + imperfect subjunctive.
  Pair with a present-subjunctive sentence so the backshift is visible.
- **English-speaker error:** leaving the subordinate verb in the present
  subjunctive after a past-tense main verb.
- **Glossing:** tag as `(impf-subj)`; note the `-ra`/`-se` alternation in the
  vocabulary table if both appear.

## Ser vs estar

- **Force into the story:** the same adjective under both — `es aburrido` /
  `está aburrido`, `es listo` / `está listo` — where the meaning genuinely
  shifts. Location of things (`estar`) against location of events (`ser`), which
  reverses the usual intuition.
- **English-speaker error:** the permanent/temporary shorthand, which fails on
  `está muerto` and `es joven`. Frame it as identity-and-definition versus
  state-and-condition instead.
- **Glossing:** both are `is`, so the distinction is invisible unless you tag
  them: `is(ser)` / `is(estar)`. This is one of the clearest cases for tags.

## Por vs para

- **Force into the story:** journeys and motives — `por` for cause, exchange,
  duration, and movement through; `para` for purpose, destination, deadline, and
  recipient. Get a minimal pair in if you can (`lo hice por ti` / `lo hice para
  ti`), since it settles the question faster than any explanation.
- **English-speaker error:** both are "for", so learners guess. Anchor `por` to
  what came before — the cause, the reason, the route — and `para` to what comes
  after: the goal, the endpoint.
- **Glossing:** gloss them as `for(cause)` and `for(purpose)`.

## Object pronouns and clitics

- **Force into the story:** dialogue and gift-giving, where indirect and direct
  objects stack. Include `se lo dio`, pronoun attachment to infinitives and
  gerunds (`quiero dárselo`), and clitic doubling (`le di el libro a María`).
- **English-speaker error:** English word order (*di le*), and dropping the
  redundant `le` in doubled constructions, which is obligatory in Spanish.
- **Glossing:** the literal line does the heavy lifting here, since
  `to-him it [I]-gave` shows the ordering directly. For attached pronouns,
  hyphenate the whole cluster: `dárselo` → `to-give-to-him-it`.

## Reflexives and the many uses of `se`

- **Force into the story:** true reflexives (`se lavó`), reciprocals (`se
  miraron`), the meaning-changing pairs (`ir` / `irse`, `dormir` / `dormirse`),
  impersonal `se dice`, and accidental `se me cayó`.
- **English-speaker error:** treating all `se` as reflexive, and avoiding the
  accidental construction in favor of a blaming direct statement.
- **Glossing:** keep `se` distinct by function — `himself`, `each-other`,
  `[one]`, or `on-me` for the accidental — rather than glossing every instance
  the same way.

## Commands and the imperative

- **Force into the story:** instructions, warnings, recipes, a character bossing
  another around. Affirmative and negative forms of the same verb side by side
  (`hazlo` / `no lo hagas`), since that's where the pattern breaks.
- **English-speaker error:** keeping the affirmative form when negating, and
  misplacing attached pronouns.
- **Glossing:** `hazlo` → `do-it`; `no lo hagas` → `not it [you]-do(subj)`,
  which shows why the negative looks like the subjunctive — because it is.

## Vocabulary or theme focus

Sometimes the focus isn't grammar but a field: cooking, medicine, a trade, the
vocabulary from the student profile. Then the story should put each target word
in a context that makes its meaning inferable without the gloss, and reuse it
once in a different context — a word met twice in different frames is retained
far better than one met twice in the same frame. Keep the grammar at level and
unremarkable so attention stays on the lexis.

## Combining two focuses

Two is the limit; three produces a story that serves neither. Pick a combination
that interacts naturally rather than two unrelated points — past tenses with
object pronouns works because narration needs both; subjunctive with por/para
doesn't, and the story will visibly strain.

Give the primary focus roughly two thirds of the weight and let the second
appear where it fits. In the grammar notes, treat them in separate blocks, and
if there is a place in the story where the two genuinely interact, point at it —
that sentence is the most valuable one in the lesson.
