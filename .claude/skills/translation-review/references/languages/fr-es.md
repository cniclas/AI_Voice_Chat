# Reading French into Spanish — comprehension traps

Where French→Spanish translation goes wrong, and what each failure looks like in
a Spanish transcript. Read this alongside `references/judging.md`, which decides
whether a deviation is a finding at all.

Each entry gives the trap, the transcript symptom, and the verdict it usually
earns. Verdicts follow the three-way split: **Missed** (meaning wrong),
**Blurred** (distinction lost), **Check** (Spanish can't show it).

This pair has a particular hazard the Spanish→English pair does not: the two
languages are close enough that a wrong reading still produces a fluent,
plausible Spanish sentence. The student is not stuck, so nothing announces the
error — which means the alignment against the answer key is doing more work here
than usual.

## Participants: who did what to whom

- **`y` and `en` dropped.** Spanish has no equivalent clitics, so a student
  reading *J'en ai trois* or *Il y va demain* often renders the sentence without
  them: "tengo tres", "va mañana". **Symptom:** a Spanish sentence that is
  grammatical but has lost the thing or place being referred back to.
  **Blurred**, or **Missed** where the referent was the point of the sentence.
- **`en` as a partitive versus `en` as `de` + noun.** *Il en parle* is "habla de
  ello", not "habla de ellos" or "lo habla". **Symptom:** the wrong preposition
  or a direct object where French has an oblique. **Missed.**
- **`lui` read as a subject.** *Lui, il ne savait rien* is a dislocated "él, él
  no sabía nada"; *Il lui a donné* is "le dio". A student who takes `lui` as the
  stressed pronoun in both places flips the participants. **Missed.**
- **Doubled pronoun order.** French `il le lui a donné` puts the direct object
  first, Spanish `se lo dio` puts the indirect first. A word-order-faithful
  reading produces *lo le dio* or swaps the roles. **Symptom:** the right two
  participants in the wrong slots. **Missed** when the roles flip; **Blurred**
  when only the order is odd.
- **`on`.** *On dit que…* is impersonal — "se dice que". Students read it as
  "nosotros" (which it also means colloquially) and assert a first-person plural
  the text never made. **Symptom:** "decimos"/"vamos" where the French is
  impersonal. **Blurred**, or **Missed** if it changes who is speaking.
- **`se` that isn't reflexive.** French pronominal verbs cover the same range as
  Spanish `se`, but the members differ: *se douter* is "sospechar", not "dudarse";
  *s'attendre à* is "esperar". **Missed.**
- **Silent subject agreement.** French never drops the subject and Spanish
  usually does, so a student translating pronoun-for-pronoun writes a stiff
  "él … él … él". Not an error — that is style, not comprehension. **Never a
  finding.**

## Tense, aspect, and mood

- **Passé composé mapped onto the wrong Spanish past.** *Il a mangé* covers both
  "comió" and "ha comido", and *Hier il a mangé* is "ayer comió" in most of the
  Spanish-speaking world. Choosing either is usually a **Check**, not an error:
  ask whether the sentence is anchored to a finished time. It becomes **Blurred**
  when the lesson is drilling aspect and the imparfait/passé composé contrast in
  the same sentence has been flattened to one Spanish tense.
- **Imparfait read as pretérito.** The mapping to *imperfecto* is nearly
  one-to-one, so an error here is real rather than structural. **Symptom:**
  background description rendered as a completed event. **Blurred**, or
  **Missed** when the story's sequence changes.
- **Plus-que-parfait flattened.** *Il avait construit* is "había construido", not
  "construyó". Flattening loses the ordering that the sentence exists to
  establish. **Missed** when the order of events matters.
- **Conditional read as future.** *Il a dit qu'il viendrait* is "dijo que
  vendría". Rendering it as "vendrá" moves the promise into the present.
  **Missed.**
- **Subjunctive triggers that differ.** French takes the indicative after
  `après que` and `espérer que` where Spanish takes the subjunctive, and the
  reverse for `bien que`/`aunque` depending on assertion. A student translating
  mood-for-mood is not making a comprehension error, but a student who reads
  `bien qu'il soit` as a statement of fact is. **Check** unless the meaning moved.
- **`si` + imparfait.** *Si j'avais le temps* is "si tuviera tiempo" —
  subjunctive in Spanish, indicative in French. Rendering it as "si tenía tiempo"
  turns a hypothetical into a habit. **Missed.**
- **`depuis` with the present.** *J'habite ici depuis trois ans* is "vivo aquí
  desde hace tres años". A past tense in the Spanish ("viví") says they have
  left. **Missed.**
- **`venir de` + infinitive.** *Il vient de partir* is "acaba de irse", not
  "viene de irse" or "va a irse". **Missed.**

## Words that look like Spanish and aren't

The largest single category for this pair, and the one where a fluent-sounding
Spanish sentence hides a flat contradiction. All **Missed** unless the sentence
survives the substitution.

| French | Actually means | Student usually says |
|---|---|---|
| quitter | dejar, abandonar | quitar |
| rester | quedarse | restar |
| entendre | oír | entender |
| attendre | esperar | atender |
| demander | pedir, preguntar | demandar |
| salir | ensuciar | salir |
| subir | sufrir, padecer | subir |
| large | ancho | largo |
| long | largo | luengo/largo (confusion with *large*) |
| gâteau | pastel | gato |
| carte | mapa, tarjeta, menú | carta (letter) |
| lettre | carta | letra |
| nombre | número | nombre (name) |
| tirer | disparar, tirar de | tirar (throw away) |
| enlever | quitar | llevar, elevar |
| ôter | quitar | otear |
| débile | idiota, endeble | débil |
| espérer | esperar (hope), *not* wait | esperar (wait) |
| assister à | asistir a, presenciar | asistir (help) |
| exprès | a propósito | expreso |
| bureau | oficina, escritorio | buró |
| journée | día (entero) | jornada laboral |
| rentrer | volver a casa | rentar |
| coin | rincón, esquina | coyuntura |

Watch the *directional* ones especially: `entendre`/`entender` and
`attendre`/`atender` are both real French words and real Spanish words with
crossed meanings, so neither the transcript nor the student's ear flags anything.

## Distinctions Spanish simply doesn't have

- **The partitive.** *Je mange du pain* is "como pan"; there is nothing in the
  Spanish to show that `du` was there. Never a finding on its own — ask instead
  whether they saw the article. **Check.**
- **`tu` / `vous`.** Spanish has `tú`/`usted`, but the line falls in a different
  place, and plural `vous` has no singular/plural cue in Latin American Spanish
  ("ustedes" covers both registers). A student rendering `vous` as `tú` may have
  read it correctly as a plural. **Check.**
- **`ne … que`.** "Only", not a negation. If they render *Je n'ai que dix euros*
  as "no tengo diez euros" the meaning has inverted. **Missed.** If they render
  it as "solo tengo diez euros", that is correct and complete.
- **Gender mismatches across the two languages.** *la mer* / *el mar*, *le lait*
  / *la leche*. Invisible in a Spanish transcript and never a comprehension
  error. **Never a finding.**

## Idioms that punish literal reading

- **`il y a` for both existence and time.** *Il y a du pain* is "hay pan";
  *il y a trois ans* is "hace tres años". Reading the second as "hay tres años"
  is **Missed**.
- **`faire` for weather and activities.** *Il fait froid* is "hace frío" — this
  one transfers perfectly and is never a finding. But *faire du vélo*, *faire les
  courses* do not: "hacer bicicleta" is not Spanish. **Blurred.**
- **`avoir` for states.** *avoir faim*, *avoir vingt ans*, *avoir besoin de* —
  all mirror Spanish `tener`, so they transfer. Flag only *avoir beau* + verb
  ("por más que…"), which does not. **Missed.**
- **`manquer`.** *Tu me manques* is "te echo de menos" — the subject and object
  are the reverse of what a Spanish speaker expects, in the opposite direction to
  `gustar`. **Symptom:** "me faltas" or the participants swapped. **Missed.**
- **`arriver` for happening.** *Qu'est-ce qui est arrivé ?* is "qué pasó", not
  "quién llegó". **Missed.**

## Structure

- **Adjective position.** French mostly postposes like Spanish, but the small
  preposed set shifts meaning the same way: *un grand homme* / *un homme grand*
  maps onto *un gran hombre* / *un hombre grande*. Getting it backwards is
  **Missed**; the parallel with Spanish means a student who knows one knows both.
- **`c'est` versus `il est`.** *C'est un médecin* / *Il est médecin* are both
  "es médico", so nothing shows in the transcript. **Never a finding.**
- **Dislocation.** *Ce livre, je l'ai lu* is emphasis, not a separate clause.
  Spanish does the same thing, so it usually transfers; flag only a student who
  turns it into two sentences with different subjects. **Blurred.**
- **`dont`.** *Le livre dont je parle* is "el libro del que hablo". A student who
  drops the preposition ("el libro que hablo") has lost the relation. **Missed.**
- **Negation with `personne`, `rien`, `jamais`.** French drops `pas` when one of
  these is present; Spanish keeps `no` before the verb. Rendering *Personne n'est
  venu* as "nadie no vino" is a calque, not a comprehension error. **Never a
  finding.** Rendering it as "alguien vino" is **Missed**.

## Adding another pair

Traps are directional. To add one, write
`references/languages/<target>-<native>.md` on the plan above, prioritizing what
the target marks that the native language doesn't. For a close pair like this
one, spend the space on false friends and on the places where a wrong reading
still produces a fluent sentence — those are the errors nothing else catches.
