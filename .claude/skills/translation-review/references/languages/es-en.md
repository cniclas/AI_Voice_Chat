# Reading Spanish into English — comprehension traps

Where Spanish→English translation goes wrong, and what each failure looks like in
an English transcript. Read this alongside `references/judging.md`, which decides
whether a deviation is a finding at all.

Each entry gives the trap, the transcript symptom, and the verdict it usually
earns. Verdicts follow the three-way split: **Missed** (meaning wrong),
**Blurred** (distinction lost), **Check** (English can't show it).

## Participants: who did what to whom

The densest source of real errors, because Spanish marks participants with small
unstressed words that a learner skims.

- **`le` / `lo` / `la` confusion.** *Le vendió la casa* is "sold the house **to**
  him"; a student reading `le` as a direct object produces "sold him" as the thing
  sold, or flips who is selling. **Symptom:** the transcript has the right verb and
  nouns but the wrong participant in the wrong role. **Missed.**
- **`se lo dio`.** Two pronouns stacked, the first replacing `le`. Students who
  learned `se` as reflexive read this as "gave it to himself". **Missed.**
- **Clitic doubling.** *Le di el libro a María* has both `le` and *a María*
  pointing at the same person. Learners sometimes invent a second recipient.
  **Missed.**
- **Dropped subjects.** Spanish carries person in the verb ending, so a whole
  paragraph can run without a subject pronoun. A student who loses the thread
  switches participants mid-story. **Symptom:** "he" and "she" swapping across
  sentences with no basis in the text. **Missed** — and note which verb ending
  they lost, since that is the fixable part.
- **`usted` read as third person.** *Usted sabe* is "you know", not "he knows".
  **Symptom:** a character narrating in third person where the Spanish is
  addressing someone. **Missed.**
- **Accidental `se`.** *Se me cayó el vaso* is "I dropped the glass" with the
  blame structurally removed — literally "the glass fell on me". Students render
  it as a plain transitive or invent an agent. **Blurred**, unless the participant
  is wrong.
- **`gustar`-type inversion.** *Me gusta el libro* has "the book" as subject.
  Most students handle this fine from habit; watch it with unfamiliar verbs of the
  same class — *me falta*, *me sobra*, *me queda*. **Missed** when the roles flip.

## Tense, aspect, and mood

- **Preterite/imperfect flattened to simple past.** English has one unmarked past
  tense, so "he told me" covers both *contó* and *contaba*. Usually a **Check**,
  not an error: ask whether it was one occasion or a habit. It becomes **Blurred**
  when the lesson focus is this contrast and the student flattens *every*
  instance — that pattern suggests they are not seeing the distinction at all.
- **Subjunctive read as indicative.** English lost most of its subjunctive, so
  *espero que venga* and *sé que viene* both surface as "comes". The mood itself
  is invisible in translation. **Check** — ask what the speaker's attitude was:
  is this something asserted, or wanted and not yet real?
- **Counterfactuals collapsed.** *Si hubiera sabido, no habría ido* rendered as
  "if I knew, I wouldn't go" loses the pastness and the fact that it did happen.
  **Missed** — the meaning genuinely changes.
- **Future-as-conjecture.** *Serán las tres* means "it must be about three", not
  "it will be three". **Symptom:** a stray prediction where the text is guessing
  about the present. **Missed**, and always worth explaining — it surprises people.
- **Conditional as future-in-the-past.** *Dijo que vendría* is "said he would
  come". Students who only know the conditional as hypothetical produce "said he
  would come if..." and hunt for a condition that isn't there. **Missed.**
- **`-ía` endings misread.** The imperfect of `-er`/`-ir` verbs and the
  conditional of every verb both end in `-ía`. *Comía* is "was eating";
  *comería* is "would eat". **Symptom:** a hypothetical appearing in a plain
  narrative passage, or vice versa. **Missed.**
- **Pluperfect flattened.** *Había terminado* rendered as "finished" loses the
  ordering of events. **Blurred**, or **Missed** if the sequence matters to the
  plot.

## Distinctions English simply doesn't have

These are structurally invisible in translation. Do not mark them wrong — ask.

- **`ser` vs `estar`.** Both are "is". *Es aburrido* (boring) and *está aburrido*
  (bored) differ in meaning, and a student can produce the wrong English while
  reading the right Spanish, or the reverse. **Check**, and only when the sentence
  actually turns on it.
- **`por` vs `para`.** Both are "for". A student translating *lo hice por ti* as
  "I did it for you" is correct — and so is the same rendering of *para ti*.
  **Check**; ask whether it was the reason or the beneficiary.
- **`tú` vs `usted`.** English lost the distinction entirely. Only worth raising
  if the story turns on formality, in which case it is a **Check**.
- **Diminutives.** *Casita*, *ahorita* carry affection or hedging that "little
  house" only half conveys. Worth a note, not a finding.

## Idioms that punish literal reading

- **`hacer` for weather** — *hace frío* is "it's cold", not "it makes cold".
  Students who over-trust the literal gloss produce the strange version; that is
  them showing their working, not an error. Say so and move on.
- **`tener` for states** — *tengo hambre / sed / veinte años / miedo* are have-
  constructions in Spanish and be-constructions in English. A student saying "I
  have twenty years" understood it fine. Not a finding.
- **`hay que` / `tener que` / `deber`.** *Hay que salir* is impersonal ("one must
  leave"); *tiene que salir* is specific. **Symptom:** an invented subject on an
  impersonal statement. **Blurred**.
- **`llevar` + time** — *llevo dos años aquí* is "I've been here two years".
  Rendered as "I carry two years", they parsed the words and not the
  construction. **Missed**, but explain the pattern rather than the sentence.
- **`acabar de`** — *acaba de llegar* is "has just arrived", not "finishes
  arriving". **Missed.**

## Structure

- **Adjective position.** *Un hombre grande* is a big man; *un gran hombre* is a
  great man. Same for *viejo amigo* (long-standing) against *amigo viejo* (aged),
  *pobre hombre* (pitiable) against *hombre pobre* (penniless). **Missed** —
  and a satisfying one to explain, since the rule is compact.
- **Double negatives.** *No vi nada* is "I saw nothing", not "I didn't see
  nothing" reversed into "I saw something". **Missed** when the polarity flips.
- **Neuter `lo`.** *Lo importante*, *lo que dijo* — abstracts a quality or a
  proposition rather than pointing at an object. **Symptom:** an invented noun
  ("the important thing" is right; "the important man" is not). **Missed.**
- **Relative `que` ambiguity.** *El hermano de mi vecina que vive en Madrid* —
  who lives in Madrid? Spanish is genuinely ambiguous here and so is the English.
  Not a finding; if the story depends on it, it's a **Check**.
- **Reflexives that change meaning.** *Ir* / *irse* (go / leave), *dormir* /
  *dormirse* (sleep / fall asleep), *quedar* / *quedarse* (arrange / stay),
  *poner* / *ponerse* (put / become). **Symptom:** the plain verb's meaning in a
  sentence where the reflexive changes it. **Missed.**

## Adding another pair

Traps are directional: what a Spanish speaker misreads in French is not what an
English speaker misreads in Spanish, so the file is named for the pair, not the
language. If the student is reading a language into a native language with no
file here, write `references/languages/<target>-<native>.md` on the same plan:
participant-marking traps, tense/aspect/mood, false friends *for that native
language*, distinctions the native language cannot show, idioms that punish
literal reading, and structural gotchas. Keep the verdict labels, since they are
what the review's shape depends on. Prioritize by what the target marks that the
native language doesn't — that is where invisible errors hide, and where Check
questions do the most work.
